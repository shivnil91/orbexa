# Launch plan

Draft for the founding team. Nothing here has been sent, posted, or spent.

The goal of the first push is a market that can be walked on a phone: a buyer pays for a draft, a seller publishes a skill, and a second buyer can hire that new listing. United States only. The three beachheads are customer replies, bookkeeping close checklists, and practical checks or plans from a trade or a hobby. Tender writing is not the pitch.

## First 50 sellers

Recruit people who already do the job by hand and can describe it in a paragraph. Do not lead with engineers who want to build agents.

1. **Bookkeepers (about 20).** Independent bookkeepers who close the books for shops. Ask them for the checklist they already send clients at month end. Offer to sit with them for twenty minutes and publish it on the call. Beachhead: Atlanta, Austin, and other cities where the founder can do the first ten in person or on a video call.
2. **Trades and retired practitioners (about 15).** Retired engineers, estimators, and senior techs. The drawing-check listing is the sample. One conversation: “What do you refuse to invent?” That sentence becomes the instruction.
3. **Hobby practitioners with a real audience (about 15).** Youth coaches, rideshare drivers who teach something on the side, community-college adjuncts. Luis is the pattern. The bar is a plan a stranger can follow, not a license.

Channels, in the order to use them:

- Warm introductions from the founder’s first ten conversations. Each published seller is asked for two names.
- Local bookkeeping and small-business bookkeeping communities, SCORE and SBDC office hours, and community-college continuing-education lists. Show up with the phone flow, not a deck.
- Nextdoor and city Facebook groups only after the listing preview is something a non-technical person can finish. Post the seller promise and a link to the earn flow. Do not buy ads until 20 sellers have published without the founder sitting next to them.

What they are offered: 80% of the starting price when a buyer accepts, a drafted listing, and a preview of the buyer screen. Say plainly that this demo does not pay a bank. The first real payouts wait on Stripe test onboarding becoming live, which is a later step and is not promised in the copy.

## First 200 buyers

Buyers are the people who would have texted the seller. Recruit them against the listings that exist, not against a category slogan.

1. **The sellers’ own clients (the first 80).** Each of the first 20 sellers names four people who already ask them for this work. The message is the job, the price, and the minutes. Maya’s catering reply and Andre’s month-end checklist are the scripts.
2. **Small-business operators (the next 80).** Bakery, HVAC, and other local service owners. Ops managers at firms of about 10 to 50 people. One need per message: a customer reply, a new-hire checklist, or a scope note. Derek and Priya are the scripts.
3. **Freelancers who resell the draft (the last 40).** Designers and consultants who need a scope note before they quote. They are buyers first. Some of them will become sellers.

Channels: the seller’s own email or text, local small-business associations, and founder-sent notes. No marketplace ads until a stranger can finish a hire on a phone without a walkthrough. The persona suite is that check.

## Referral loop

Keep it to one sentence a person can forward.

- A seller who publishes gets a personal link. A buyer who pays through that link is tagged to the seller.
- After a buyer accepts a draft, the screen asks for one person who has the same job. The message they forward is the job title, the price, and the sample, not a description of Orbexa.
- A buyer who pays, and whose forwarded link produces a paid job, gets the next draft at the platform fee waived (they pay the seller’s 80% only). A seller whose link produces a new published seller gets their next accepted job at a 0% platform fee. Cap both at one credit until the ledger can show the waiver. Do not invent a balance in the product until that credit exists in the order.

The loop is supply to demand (seller to their client) and demand to supply (a buyer who knows a practitioner). Track both as counts, not as a viral coefficient we do not have yet.

## What to show a VC

Report these from real orders. Do not fill them with example listings. Example jobs are labelled and are not GMV.

- **GMV.** Sum of prices on accepted drafts, in dollars, for the week. Separate example-listing volume from volume on listings a person published.
- **Liquidity.** Of the needs typed in the week, the share that produced a paid draft, and the share of published listings that received at least one paid order. A marketplace with sellers and no buyers is not liquid.
- **Repeat rate.** Buyers who paid a second time, over buyers who paid at least once. Sellers who received a second accepted order, over sellers who received one.
- **Take rate.** 20% of the starting price on the simple draft. State it as the contracted share, and show the dollars actually retained on accepted orders. Do not average in expert-tier fees until those tiers are on the simple path.

Alongside those four, show the operating counts that explain them: sellers published, buyers who paid, median time from “show my matches” to the draft on screen, and the share of persona walks that finish in three steps. The phone suite is the regression for the last one.

Stop the raise conversation if liquidity is zero. A take rate on no volume is not a business.
